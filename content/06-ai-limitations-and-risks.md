
# Section 6: Limitations and Risks of Artificial Intelligence

## 1. Learning Objectives

After completing this section, learners should be able to:

1. Explain why AI systems may produce incorrect or misleading outputs.
2. Define AI hallucination and distinguish it from a verified fact.
3. Describe how bias can lead to unfair results.
4. Identify privacy and data protection risks.
5. Explain the difference between accuracy and reliability.
6. Apply a verification process to evaluate AI-generated information.
7. Recommend practical steps for reducing common AI risks.

## 2. Introduction: Why Should We Question AI Outputs?

AI systems can be useful for learning, research, business, and creative work. However, an AI-generated answer is not automatically correct simply because it sounds confident or is presented clearly.

An AI system may misunderstand a question, use incomplete information, produce a false statement, or perform poorly in a situation different from the ones it was designed for.

An **AI risk** is the possibility that using an AI system could lead to a negative outcome, such as misinformation, unfair treatment, privacy loss, or a safety problem.

A **limitation** is a weakness or boundary that affects what a system can do reliably.

Understanding these risks helps people decide when AI is appropriate, how its results should be checked, and when human expertise is necessary.

## 3. Incorrect or Misleading Outputs

### 3.1 What Are Incorrect Outputs?

An incorrect output is information or a result that does not accurately answer the question or perform the intended task.

AI systems can produce errors for several reasons:

- The information provided to the system is incomplete or inaccurate.
- The system misunderstands the question or its context.
- The task is outside the system's capabilities.
- The system uses outdated information.
- The system produces a plausible response without adequately verifying its claims.

A misleading output may also contain some correct information but present it in a way that gives the reader the wrong impression.

### 3.2 Example

A student asks an AI chatbot to explain a scientific concept. The explanation contains three correct statements and one incorrect claim.

Because most of the explanation is correct, the student may assume the entire answer is reliable.

This demonstrates why checking only the general appearance of an answer is not enough. Individual claims may need to be verified.

### 3.3 How to Reduce This Risk

- Compare important claims with reliable sources.
- Check calculations, dates, names, quotations, and references.
- Look for evidence that supports the answer.
- Ask a teacher or qualified professional when the consequences of an error could be serious.
- Avoid treating confidence or fluent writing as proof of accuracy.

## 4. AI Hallucinations

### 4.1 Definition

An **AI hallucination** is an output in which an AI system presents information that is false, fabricated, or unsupported as though it were valid information.

Hallucinations are particularly important in generative AI, which creates text, images, code, and other content.

For example, a chatbot might invent a research paper title, provide a reference that does not exist, or give an incorrect explanation of a mathematical formula.

The term does not mean that the system is experiencing a human mental experience. It describes a type of unreliable AI output.

### 4.2 Worked Example: Checking an AI-Generated Reference

Imagine that an AI chatbot gives a student this fictional reference:

> Example: A. Researcher (2022), "Artificial Intelligence in Every Classroom," Journal of Educational Machines.

The student does not know whether this source exists.

The student should follow these steps:

1. **Identify the claim.** The chatbot is claiming that a particular paper was published.
2. **Search for the exact title.** Look in a reliable academic search service, library catalogue, or publisher website.
3. **Check the author and publication details.** Confirm that the author, journal, year, and other details match.
4. **Open the source.** If a matching source is found, read it to check whether it supports the claim.
5. **Decide what to do.** If the reference cannot be verified, do not cite it as a real source. Find a genuine source that supports the point instead.

This example is fictional and is intended only to demonstrate the checking process.

### 4.3 Important Lesson

Finding a webpage with a similar title does not automatically prove that an AI-generated reference is correct. The actual publication and its contents must be checked.

## 5. Bias and Unfair Results

### 5.1 What Is AI Bias?

**Bias** refers to a systematic pattern that can lead to unbalanced or unfair results.

AI bias may arise from:

- Training data that does not adequately represent the people affected.
- Historical inequalities reflected in existing records.
- Choices made when designing or evaluating a system.
- Using an AI system in a context for which it was not properly tested.

Bias does not require a computer to have an intention to discriminate. A system can produce unfair results because of its data, design, or use.

### 5.2 Worked Example: An AI Hiring System

Imagine that a company uses an AI system to help rank job applications.

The system was trained on historical hiring records that mostly represented successful applicants from one background. It gives lower rankings to some applicants with different backgrounds.

A responsible evaluation should ask:

1. Does the training data represent the range of applicants?
2. Does the system perform differently across relevant groups?
3. Are the ranking criteria appropriate for the job?
4. Can a human reviewer identify and challenge an unfair result?
5. Should the system be changed or stopped until the issue is addressed?

The company should investigate the results, evaluate its data and criteria, and provide appropriate human oversight.

The fact that an AI system produces a numerical score does not mean that the score is fair or objective.

### 5.3 Reducing Bias

Possible approaches include:

- Using relevant and representative data.
- Testing results across different groups.
- Reviewing the criteria used by the system.
- Monitoring performance after deployment.
- Providing human review and ways to challenge important decisions.

These measures can reduce risk but cannot guarantee that every form of bias will be eliminated.

## 6. Privacy and Data Protection

### 6.1 What Is Personal Information?

**Personal information** is information that identifies a person or can reasonably be linked to them. Examples may include a name, student number, photograph, address, or identifiable health record.

**Privacy** concerns how information about people is collected, used, stored, and shared.

Some AI tools process user inputs or retain information according to their specific settings and policies. Users should not assume that every tool handles information in the same way.

### 6.2 Example: Uploading a Student Record

A student wants an AI tool to summarize class performance and uploads a spreadsheet containing classmates' names, student numbers, and grades.

Possible risks include:

- Exposing information to people who should not see it.
- Using a service that is not approved by the school.
- Sharing more information than the task requires.
- Violating school rules or applicable privacy requirements.

### 6.3 Safer Practices

Before providing information to an AI tool:

1. Check whether you have permission to use the information.
2. Check the tool's privacy policy and settings.
3. Remove unnecessary personal details.
4. Use an approved tool for sensitive or confidential information.
5. Follow applicable school and organizational rules.

Removing names does not always make data anonymous. Other details, especially when combined, may still identify a person.

## 7. Reliability and Other Limitations

### 7.1 Accuracy and Reliability

**Accuracy** describes how close a result is to the correct or accepted value.

**Reliability** describes how consistently a system performs as expected under specified conditions.

These concepts are related but different. A system might produce accurate results in some situations but perform inconsistently in others.

For example, a navigation system may estimate travel times reasonably well under normal conditions but perform poorly when a road is suddenly closed.

### 7.2 Context and Changing Information

An AI system may not have all the information needed for a particular situation. Information can also change over time.

Examples include:

- Outdated regulations or university deadlines.
- A scientific claim that has since been revised.
- A navigation recommendation that does not reflect a new road closure.
- A recommendation that ignores a user's important circumstances.

For time-sensitive or location-specific information, check an appropriate current source.

### 7.3 Limited Context and Transparency

Some AI systems may not fully account for the social, cultural, or practical context of a question. Some systems also provide limited explanations of how their outputs were produced.

**Transparency** means making relevant information about a system, its purpose, and its limitations understandable to the people who need it.

When a result is important, users should understand what the system is intended to do, what it cannot reliably do, and who is responsible for reviewing its output.

### 7.4 Overreliance on AI

**Overreliance** occurs when people trust or depend on AI more than is justified.

Examples include copying an AI-generated essay without checking its claims, accepting an automated recommendation without considering relevant evidence, or assuming that an AI tool must be correct because it is advanced.

A responsible user treats AI as a tool that may help with a task, not as an automatic replacement for knowledge, judgment, or accountability.

## 9. Worked Example: Verifying a Scientific Claim

An AI chatbot states:

"All AI systems are trained using exactly the same method."

A student wants to use this statement in a report.

### Step 1: Identify the claim

The statement makes a universal claim about every AI system.

### Step 2: Ask what evidence is needed

The student needs a reliable source explaining different types of AI systems and their methods.

### Step 3: Consult reliable sources

The student checks educational or technical sources describing different AI approaches. These show that AI systems can use different methods, including rule-based approaches and machine-learning methods.

### Step 4: Compare the evidence

The statement is too broad because AI systems do not all use exactly the same method.

### Step 5: Rewrite the claim

A more accurate version is:

"AI systems use different approaches depending on their design and purpose. Some use predefined rules, while others use machine-learning methods."

### Step 6: Cite the source

The student records the actual source consulted and ensures that it supports the revised statement.

**Lesson:** Verification does not simply mean finding a source that agrees with a claim. It means evaluating the evidence and changing the claim when the evidence does not support it.

## 10. Risk Assessment Table

Use this table to evaluate common AI risks.

| Risk | Example | Possible consequence | Risk-reduction measure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Incorrect output | A chatbot gives a wrong scientific explanation. | Learners develop misunderstandings. | Check against reliable sources. |
| Hallucination | A chatbot invents a research reference. | A report cites a nonexistent source. | Verify the publication and its contents. |
| Bias | A hiring system unfairly ranks a group of applicants. | Unfair opportunities or treatment. | Test performance across groups and review criteria. |
| Privacy | A user uploads confidential student records. | Personal information may be exposed. | Minimize data and use approved tools. |
| Unreliability | A prediction performs poorly under changing conditions. | Decisions are based on a poor estimate. | Test, monitor, and reassess performance. |
| Overreliance | A student copies an answer without checking it. | Errors remain undetected and learning is reduced. | Verify information and explain the reasoning independently. |

The appropriate response depends on both the likelihood of a problem and the seriousness of its possible consequences.

## 13. Answer Key and Explanations

### Activity A

1. B — AI hallucination. The system has presented an invented reference as though it were real.
2. C — Bias or unfairness. The results may systematically disadvantage a group.
3. D — Privacy risk. Identifiable and potentially confidential information has been shared with a tool that has not been approved for this purpose.
4. A — Incorrect or misleading output. The explanation contains false information.
5. E — Reliability or overreliance. The user trusts a prediction without considering changing conditions.

6. Accuracy concerns how close a result is to the correct value. Reliability concerns whether a system performs consistently as expected under specified conditions.
7. Example answers: Compare the claim with a reliable source; open and read the cited source; check the date and author; consult an appropriate expert.
8. Other details may identify the person, especially when combined with other information.
9. Example answers: Use representative data, test outcomes across groups, review system criteria, and provide human oversight.
10. AI can make mistakes or miss important context. Human review helps evaluate the evidence, recognize limitations, and take responsibility for decisions.

### Activity B

1. The chatbot incorrectly claims that AI answers do not need to be checked.
2. AI systems can produce incorrect or unsupported information. Their outputs are not guaranteed to be correct.
3. Example response: "AI can help you find ideas and understand information, but you should verify factual claims against reliable sources and follow your institution's rules for using AI."
4. Example answers: Verify factual claims and references; check whether the source supports the claim; confirm dates and details; review the course's AI-use policy.

Other answers are acceptable if they are accurate and supported by clear reasoning.

## 14. Key Takeaways

- AI systems can produce incorrect, misleading, or unsupported information.
- Hallucinations may include invented facts or references.
- Bias can result from data, design choices, or inappropriate use.
- Personal information should be handled carefully when using AI tools.
- Accuracy and reliability are related but distinct.
- AI performance may vary across tasks and conditions.
- Important information should be checked using reliable evidence.
- Human oversight is particularly important when errors could have serious consequences.
